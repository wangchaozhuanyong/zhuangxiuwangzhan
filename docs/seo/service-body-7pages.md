<p>Paint lasts as long as the preparation underneath it. The first visit is spent looking at the wall rather than the colour chart: hairline cracks, damp patches, peeling or chalky old paint, hollow plaster, and any area that has been patched before. <p><strong>Colour and sheen</strong></p>
<p>Colour is confirmed against a code or a physical sample rather than a screen, because screens shift tone. <p><strong>Quotation</strong></p>
<p>The quotation states the number of coats, whether primer is included, how much surface repair is covered, and what protection is provided. Additional coats needed because the existing colour is much darker, or because the wall is porous, are listed as separate items rather than absorbed silently. Exterior work depends on weather and is scheduled around it rather than promised against a fixed date.</p>
<p><strong>Handover</strong></p>
<p>Walk the area in daylight, not under artificial light, and mark anything that needs touching up. <p>Tiling fails at the substrate, not at the tile. Before laying we check whether the existing floor is level, whether the old tile or screed needs removal, whether any existing tile is hollow, and whether the area is wet enough to need waterproofing underneath. Tiling over hollow tile transfers the problem to the new layer, and it usually shows up as loose or cracked tiles within a year.</p>
<p><strong>Sequence</strong></p>
<p>Hacking out old tiles and screed where required, clearing and levelling the base, then setting out the layout from a datum line so that cuts fall in the least visible positions. Adhesive is selected for the tile size — large-format tiles need a flatter substrate and a different adhesive than standard sizes. Expansion joints are kept at the perimeter, and after laying we tap-check for hollow spots and record any tile that needs replacement.</p>
<p><strong>Tile selection</strong></p>
<p>Size, finish and slip resistance matter more than pattern. Large-format tiles look calm but need a flatter floor and cost more to lay; small tiles handle slope changes in wet areas better. In bathrooms and balconies, slip resistance should be confirmed before aesthetics. Confirm the batch and shade when ordering, and keep spare pieces — the same shade will not be available in two years.</p>
<p><strong>Quotation</strong></p>
<p>The quotation separates removal, levelling, waterproofing where applicable, laying, grouting and any cutting around fixtures. Tile supply can be yours or arranged by us, and the two are priced separately. If a substrate problem is only discovered after removal, the additional work is agreed before it proceeds rather than billed afterwards.</p>
<p><strong>Handover</strong></p>
<p>Check for hollow tiles by tapping, look along the joints under raking light, and test drainage in wet areas by running water. Grout needs sealing in wet areas and re-sealing periodically. Keep two or three spare tiles; matching a discontinued shade later is difficult.</p>

<p>A ceiling hides the services above it, which is why its layout has to be settled before the frame goes up. The finished height comes first — it is determined by the air-conditioning units, the pipe runs and any structural beam. <p><strong>Sequence</strong></p>
<p>Metal framing is fixed at the correct spacing to the slab or to the wall, then plasterboard is screwed, taped and jointed before skimming. <p><strong>Design decisions</strong></p>
<p>Flat ceilings are cheaper, easier to service and read calmer in small rooms. Cove ceilings with concealed lighting add depth but need enough void for the light and the driver, and they collect dust. Light troughs should be sized around the actual fitting rather than guessed. <p><strong>Quotation</strong></p>
<p>The quotation covers framing, boarding, jointing, skimming, access panels and any cove or trough construction. Painting is usually a separate item unless stated. <p><strong>Handover</strong></p>
<p>Open every access panel and confirm it still closes flush. Check the ceiling under raking light for waviness, and confirm that the downlights sit where agreed rather than where the joists allowed. <p>A partition decides sound, privacy and services, not just the wall line. Before fixing the frame we confirm what the room is for — a meeting room, a bedroom, a consultation room or a store — because that determines whether a single plasterboard layer is enough or whether insulation and a second layer are needed. <p>Plasterboard gives privacy and takes paint well; glass gives light and sightlines but different acoustic and privacy behaviour, and needs framing that can carry the weight. <p><strong>Quotation and approval</strong></p>
<p>The quotation separates framing, boarding, insulation, finishing, and any glass or aluminium system. <p><strong>Handover</strong></p>
<p>Close the door and listen from the other side — that is the honest acoustic test. <p>Electrical and plumbing are the trades that are hardest to correct once the finishes are on. The first visit maps what exists: the incoming supply capacity, the consumer unit, which circuit serves which room, the water pressure, and where the stop valve and floor traps are. <p><strong>Sequence</strong></p>
<p>Every new or relocated point is listed and priced per point, so the scope is countable rather than estimated. First fix runs before plaster, tiles or ceiling go on: conduits and pipes are routed and fixed, then pressure-tested and the circuits tested. <p><strong>What the check often finds</strong></p>
<p>In properties over twenty years old, the usual findings are undersized incoming supply for modern loads, no earth leakage protection on some circuits, pipe joints that were repaired rather than replaced, and floor traps that were tiled over. Each of these affects what can safely be added. Where an unsafe condition is found, it is reported first; adding points to an unsafe installation only hides the problem.</p>
<p><strong>Quotation</strong></p>
<p>The quotation lists point types separately, then concealed route length, then any consumer unit or pipe upgrade. Areas that can only be assessed after opening up are marked as provisional and agreed before the additional work proceeds. Partial scopes are possible — a bathroom re-pipe, a single circuit — provided the existing installation is safe to connect to.</p>
<p><strong>Handover</strong></p>
<p>Test every socket, every switch and every tap. Run water in each outlet and check for leaks at the visible joints after a few minutes, not immediately. <p>Industrial work is judged on whether production can restart, not on how the space looks. The first conversation is about operations: what the floor has to carry, whether forklifts or heavy racking will run on it, how much air has to move and where it exhausts, what electrical capacity the machinery needs, and which areas cannot be shut down at all. Those answers determine the specification before any finish is discussed.</p>
<p><strong>Phasing</strong></p>
<p>Most factories cannot stop for weeks, so the work is split into zones. Production continues in the unaffected bays, and each zone is handed back in a usable state before the next one starts. That requires the services to be isolatable per zone, and it requires the sequence to be agreed with your production schedule rather than with the contractor's convenience.</p>
<p><strong>Technical decisions</strong></p>
<p>Floor systems, coatings and hardstanding are selected against load and traffic — a coating chosen for appearance will fail under forklift wheels. Ventilation and extraction are sized for the process, not for comfort. Where the building has fire requirements, compartmentation, detection and escape routes constrain the layout before finishes matter. Racking needs the slab checked for point loads, not just the aisle width checked for the truck.</p>
<p><strong>Quotation</strong></p>
<p>Work is priced by measured area and by item rather than as one lump sum: floor area, coating system, racking, services, and the phasing plan each appear separately, so the budget can be staged. Where an authority submission or professional certification is required, it is identified before work starts, along with who is responsible for it.</p>
<p><strong>Handover</strong></p>
<p>Each zone is tested and handed back before the next begins — power, ventilation, drainage and the floor surface under load. Keep the as-built information: which circuit feeds which machine, where the isolation points are, and what coating was applied. In a live factory that record is worth more than the drawings.</p>
